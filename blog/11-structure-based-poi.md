# Choosing a point of interest: make the rejection decision explicit

Rank potential zones using context, boundaries and a separate entry condition.

**Lesson 11 · Week 4 · About 3 minutes**

[Series index](README.md) · [Lesson worksheet](../lessons/11-structure-based-poi.md) · [Roadmap](../SOLO_LEARNING_ROADMAP.md#week-4)

Mark enough order blocks, gaps and old swings and almost any future reaction will touch something. That makes a chart easy to explain afterward and difficult to use beforehand.

A point of interest, or POI, becomes useful when it narrows a decision. You should be able to explain why you selected one candidate, rejected another and would accept no suitable zone.

## Begin with the structural question

Before drawing a new rectangle, identify the current external range, directional hypothesis and nearby opposing reference. Then ask what price interaction you are waiting to observe.

The workbook permits several kinds of POI, including OBs, FVGs, breakers and prior swings. Each brings its own formation rules. Selecting a zone from that inventory is different from proving that it will produce a reversal.

## Use mandatory conditions before a score

A simple research rubric can require that the zone was confirmed before the decision, has fixed boundaries and has a clear invalidation. Optional context might include range position, alignment with the selected structural direction and distance to an opposing objective.

If a mandatory condition fails, a high score elsewhere should not hide it. “Strong context” cannot repair an undefined zone or make a future-confirmed pattern available earlier.

[TTrades’ POI article](https://ttrades.com/the-only-points-of-interest-that-actually-matter-for-trading/) offers one ordered selection framework. Compare it with your written rubric while keeping its rules attributed to that educator.

## A hypothetical comparison

Imagine an upward research hypothesis inside a range of 100–120. Candidate A is a confirmed FVG at 107–109. Candidate B is an older candle-based zone at 103–105. Both sit below equilibrium at 110, but that shared description does not make them equivalent.

You might select A because your model requires the nearest qualifying first-revisit gap. You might reject B because its permitted retest already occurred. Those reasons are executable if the rules were written beforehand.

Alternatively, your model might select B and exclude A. What matters for this exercise is consistency, not finding a universal ranking. If price reaches A and fails before reacting at B, preserve the original selection and its outcome.

## Location is separate from confirmation

A zone touch can be logged without being an entry. If the strategy requires a completed close above a chosen minor high, no entry exists until that event occurs. If the strategy instead uses a limit order, define the placement time and fill assumptions.

Practice on fifteen preselected historical swings. Choose no more than two candidates, record the rejected alternative and permit “none.” Save the decision before advancing the chart. Your review should evaluate the selection process even when the best-performing zone was the one you did not choose.
