# Daily bias: write a hypothesis that can change your mind

A directional plan needs an objective, counterevidence and a neutral option.

**Lesson 15 · Week 5 · About 3 minutes**

[Series index](README.md) · [Lesson worksheet](../lessons/15-daily-bias.md) · [Roadmap](../SOLO_LEARNING_ROADMAP.md#week-5)

A daily bias is useful when it prepares you for a decision before the session unfolds. It becomes less useful when it turns into a prediction you feel obliged to defend.

The workbook asks for a bullish, bearish or neutral scenario, an objective and a condition that invalidates it. Those fields give you something more informative to review than whether your directional guess happened to be correct.

## Begin with known context

Mark the previous day’s extremes, confirmed larger swings, relevant dealing range and any scheduled event that affects your observation window. Use only information available at the planning time.

Then write the direction as a hypothesis: “Under these conditions, I will look for the model’s qualifying upward setup.” This says what you are willing to study. It does not require an immediate entry or imply that every local decline is wrong.

A public example is [TTrades’ daily-bias framework](https://ttrades.com/easy-daily-bias-a-mechanical-trading-framework/). Treat its candle and confirmation conventions as that educator’s model. Do not silently add them to a different strategy’s test sample.

## Separate objective from invalidation

In a hypothetical plan, yesterday’s high is 120, yesterday’s low is 100 and current price is 108. Your research model favors observing a move toward 120 while the selected context low remains valid.

The objective is a proposed destination. Invalidation is the price event that cancels the hypothesis. They answer different questions. If the rule says a completed context candle must close below 100 to invalidate the bias, an execution-timeframe wick to 99.8 does not meet that exact rule by itself.

You may still decline an entry because its own stop or setup conditions failed. That is a separate decision and belongs in the journal.

## Make neutral operational

Define when evidence is insufficient: conflicting context rules, an unconfirmed range, an event exclusion or no clear objective. A neutral classification should have reasons just as bullish and bearish classifications do.

Avoid choosing neutral only after a session proves choppy. Record it at the decision time. If your rules permit a later change, write the event and timestamp that caused it.

## Score three different things

At the end of a session, ask whether the hypothesis was supported, whether an eligible entry occurred and whether you followed the plan. A favorable directional move may offer no permitted entry. A losing simulated trade can still follow every rule.

Keep a separate judgment for your reasoning quality. Were the range anchors known? Was conflicting evidence acknowledged? Did invalidation stay fixed? Reviewing those questions helps distinguish a weak decision that happened to win from a well-specified process that encountered an ordinary loss.

Practice on ten historical sessions, stopping the chart before each session. Preserve the original note and write the review beneath it. The untouched plan is the evidence that makes the review useful.
